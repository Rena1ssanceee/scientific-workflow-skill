# V3-Style Literature Survey and Final Research Plan Report

Use this as the default structure for Mode B unless the user requests another format.

## Title

`[Research topic]: literature survey and final research-plan report`

## 1. Overall conclusion

State directly:

- the most suitable research direction;
- what the project should not be reduced to;
- the central scientific storyline;
- why it matches the existing data;
- the recommended Chinese and English titles.

This section should allow a reader to understand the final decision before reading the evidence.

## 2. Current research progress and unresolved problems

Organize by scientific development rather than paper-by-paper summaries.

Suggested subsections:

- data and study-design evolution;
- mature conventional analyses;
- current advanced analyses;
- representative studies closest to the project;
- limitations of current evidence;
- why conventional repetition is insufficient.

## 3. Main gaps relevant to the current project

List only gaps that are supported by literature and can be addressed by the available data.

For each gap explain:

- what existing studies have done;
- what remains unresolved;
- why the current data are suitable;
- the interpretation boundary.

## 4. Existing data and study conditions

Include:

- unique participant or specimen counts;
- disease and subgroup structure;
- repeated or paired timepoints;
- true chronology;
- data types and pairing across layers;
- overlapping participants and deduplication;
- group-label provenance;
- completed analyses;
- data strengths;
- material limitations.

## 5. Feasible research directions based on existing data

Present two to four candidates in prose.

For each direction include:

- scientific question;
- evidence basis;
- data use;
- expected result chain;
- advantage;
- limitation;
- feasibility judgment.

End with a comparison and explain why one direction is selected.

## 6. Final recommended plan

### 6.1 Recommended title

Chinese and English titles, plus optional alternatives.

### 6.2 Central scientific question

One paragraph defining object, process, main contrast, and intended contribution.

### 6.3 Research objectives

Normally use two or three sequential objectives rather than a long list of methods.

### 6.4 Cohort, grouping, and chronology

Define independent unit, disease groups, overlap groups, label source, paired structure, time order, exclusions, and main versus exploratory strata.

### 6.5 Analysis modules

For every module specify:

- input data;
- analytical unit;
- comparison;
- method class;
- output;
- scientific question answered.

### 6.6 Statistical design

Include paired or mixed models, covariates, interaction terms, multiple testing, missing data, small subgroup policy, sensitivity analyses, and leakage-safe validation.

### 6.7 Public-data or external validation

Explain what can be validated, dataset comparability, harmonization limits, and what cannot be externally validated.

### 6.8 Expected evidence chain and interpretation

Describe the intended sequence of results and separate direct conclusions from exploratory interpretations.

## 7. Figure plan and technical route

List main figures and supplementary figures. For each figure state the question answered and required data.

Provide a technical-route narrative or diagram specification.

## 8. Main limitations and implementation conditions

Discuss limitations that materially affect feasibility or interpretation. Avoid a long unrelated “not recommended” catalogue; explain boundaries in relation to the chosen plan.

## 9. Conclusion

Restate what should be done, why it is the best fit, how all major data are used, and the maximum defensible claim strength.

## 10. References

Include the core papers directly supporting the report. Keep the full searchable corpus in a separate literature list or evidence matrix.

## Style rules

- Use continuous scientific prose and restrained heading hierarchy.
- Avoid large tables in the main report when a narrative v3-style report is requested.
- Keep methodological detail sufficient for feasibility, but do not turn the report into a software manual.
- Use cautious evidence language.
- Keep counts, terminology, and chronology identical across all sections.
